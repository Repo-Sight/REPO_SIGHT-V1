Long and complex functions in 14 C++ projects

← Back to Learn

We scanned 14 widely used open-source C++ projects — 19,876 functions in all — and counted how many cross two common limits: more than 100 lines, or a cyclomatic complexity above 10. Only 240 functions (1.2%) are longer than 100 lines and 461 (2.3%) are above complexity 10. The typical function is tiny, with a median of 6 lines. And 64% of the long ones sit in tests, benchmarks, examples or other non-production paths.

So the headline is not that C++ is full of monster functions. It is a short tail, and most of it is where you would expect to find it.

How we measured

The projects are Catch2, CLI11, cxxopts, doctest, FTXUI, Google Benchmark, GoogleTest, LevelDB, nlohmann/json, RapidJSON, RE2, spdlog, yaml-cpp, {fmt}. Each was scanned at a pinned commit, listed in the open data file, using the same engine that powers the public scanner. Only C and C++ source and header files are counted.

  • Length is the number of lines from a function’s first line to its closing brace, as REPO-SIGHT reports it.
  • Complexity is the per-function cyclomatic complexity check, which raises a finding above 10. See cyclomatic complexity, explained for how the count works.
  • Non-production means a path with a directory named like test, tests, testing, spec, docs, examples, benchmark, fuzz, third_party or vendor, or a file named like a test.

Two caveats. Test macros such as TEST_CASE are counted as function-like blocks, because that is how they behave. And some libraries ship a single-header copy of their own code, or bundle another library, so a few long functions are counted twice. About ten of the 240 sit in such copies.

The shape of the distribution

  • The median function is 6 lines long. One function in a hundred is longer than about 120 lines.
  • 723 functions (3.6%) are over 50 lines, 240 (1.2%) are over 100, and 97 are over 200.
  • Of the 461 functions above complexity 10, 331 score 11–20, 106 score 21–50 and 24 score above 50.

Where the long functions live

154 of the 240 long functions (64%) are in non-production paths, and 128 of them are test macros: 101 TEST_CASE, 13 TEST, 11 SECTION and 3 TEST_SUITE. Frameworks like Catch2 and doctest encourage putting many assertions in one block, and a block of assertions is long without being hard to follow. The longest block in the whole data set is a TEST_CASE in nlohmann/json’s unit-bjdata.cpp, at 2,967 lines.

The tail is also very uneven between projects. Here is the share of each project’s functions that are over 100 lines:

  • nlohmann/json: 118 of 2,137 (5.52%)
  • RE2: 23 of 964 (2.39%)
  • doctest: 9 of 487 (1.85%)
  • FTXUI: 14 of 1,133 (1.24%)
  • RapidJSON: 22 of 2,132 (1.03%)
  • cxxopts: 6 of 639 (0.94%)
  • CLI11: 11 of 1,446 (0.76%)
  • Google Benchmark: 4 of 573 (0.70%)
  • Catch2: 10 of 1,574 (0.64%)
  • spdlog: 8 of 1,389 (0.58%)
  • LevelDB: 3 of 636 (0.47%)
  • {fmt}: 7 of 2,192 (0.32%)
  • yaml-cpp: 4 of 2,578 (0.16%)
  • GoogleTest: 1 of 1,996 (0.05%)

nlohmann/json alone supplies 118 of the 240 long functions (49%), mostly because of its large test suite. GoogleTest has one in nearly 2,000. The spread between projects is more than a hundredfold, which says more about how a project is organised than about its quality.

The longest production functions

Setting tests aside, the longest functions share a pattern:

  • Catch2 makeCommandLineParser, 354 lines — builds the command-line parser and its option handlers.
  • nlohmann/json pow5_128, 335 lines — a table of 128-bit approximations of powers of five in pow5_table.hpp, not logic.
  • nlohmann/json patch_inplace, 304 lines — applies a JSON patch document.
  • {fmt} format_float, 303 lines — and spdlog bundles a 297-line copy of it.
  • CLI11 from_config, 285 lines — reads configuration files.
  • RapidJSON ParseNumber, 281 lines — parses numbers from JSON text.

These are number formatting and parsing, configuration and command-line handling, and constant tables. Code that has to accept messy, varied input tends to grow branches, and code that holds a table tends to be long without being complicated.

Complexity tells a similar story

Of the 461 functions above complexity 10, 102 (22%) are in non-production paths, a smaller share than for length. The most complex production functions are again parsers: CLI11’s from_config at 111, RapidJSON’s ParseNumber at 95, CLI11’s _parse_arg at 88 and {fmt}’s parse_chrono_format at 82. Their branching follows the grammar of the input they read, so a high score here is a reason to test thoroughly rather than a reason to panic.

What to do with this

  • Read the list by path. Look at long functions in production code first. Treat test blocks as a separate, lower-priority question.
  • Ask what kind of long it is. A constant table can move into data. A parser with many branches deserves tests for each branch. A function doing several jobs should be split.
  • Watch the trend, not the zero. A handful of justified outliers is normal. A rising count between scans is the signal.

Frequently asked questions

Is 100 lines too long for a C++ function?

It is a reasonable point at which to look more closely, not a rule. In this data set 1.2% of functions pass it, and many of those are tests or tables.

Why do the numbers differ from other tools?

Counts depend on definitions: whether macros count as functions, whether single-header copies are included, and what counts as a branch. We publish ours so you can check them.

Can I run this on my own project?

Yes. Paste a GitHub URL and the report lists your longest and most complex functions by file. The C++ code analyzer page explains what is checked.
